Amplify Energy Spotify Sponsorship Analysis

Building a data-driven Spotify sponsorship strategy for an energy drink launch: turning target customer insight into a scoring model, choosing songs and artists, and planning a measurable go-to-market rollout.

  • Context Accounting Analytics and Financial Accounting course project
  • Data 600+ songs and 1,000+ artists (year-end charts, 2010–2019)
  • Tools Excel, Power Query, pivot tables

What we recommended

  1. Sponsor five songs and partner with five artists selected by a weighted scoring model that prioritizes brand fit (energy, tempo, danceability) over popularity.
  2. Split the budget roughly 70/30 between the highest-fit songs and artists and one widely recognized artist for reach, with placements focused on workout, running, and gym playlists.
  3. Launch in three phases, starting with a branded workout playlist and a small test budget before scaling through fitness creators and an in-store QR code.
  4. Measure engagement, not vanity metrics: playlist completion, cost per engaged listener, saves and adds, and QR scans per 1,000 cans.

The problem

Amplify Energy is launching an energy drink and planned to market it through song and artist sponsorships on Spotify. The question was which music to put behind the brand, and how to roll the campaign out.

The target customer is an 18–29-year-old young professional who is into fitness and high-energy activities and active on social media. Our product tests well with this group, and what they care about in music is intensity: BPM, energy, and danceability. Whether an artist is mainstream matters much less to them. That insight shaped every decision that followed.

Insight: Popular music is losing energy

Looking across year-end chart songs from 2010 to 2019, average energy fell about 17%, from roughly 78 to 64.7, while average popularity climbed to 84.4. The most popular music is increasingly the least energetic.

The brand needs music popular enough to get attention but energetic enough to match the product. Simply picking the biggest hits would work against the brand.

That tension became the design problem for the analysis: build a way to rank music that rewards brand fit first, while still giving popularity some weight where it creates real marketing value.

Approach: A weighted compatibility scoring model

We cleaned and merged the song and artist datasets in Power Query, benchmarked artists across pivot tables, and then built three scores in Excel.

1. Rank every song and artist on four variables

Each song and artist was ranked on energy, BPM, danceability, and popularity. Ranking puts all four variables on the same scale, so a tempo measured in beats per minute can be compared fairly with a 0–100 popularity score.

2. Weight the variables differently for songs and artists

  • Energy gets the most weight in both scores because it’s the trait most directly tied to an energy drink’s identity.
  • BPM is next, since tempo signals a song’s pace and intensity.
  • Danceability adds a moderate measure of how engaging and active a song feels.
  • Popularity gets just 5% for songs, so a lesser-known track can outrank a hit if it fits the brand better. For artists it rises to 15%, because a brand partnership also buys recognition, audience reach, and fan engagement.

3. Combine them into one recommendation score

The song score carries most of the weight because the campaign’s goal is music that captures the brand’s energy and atmosphere. The artist score acts as a complement, accounting for the strategic value of the person behind the song. Final picks had to perform well across all three scores, not just one.

Why this matters: The model turns a fuzzy question (“what music fits our brand?”) into a transparent, repeatable ranking, and it makes the brand-fit vs. popularity tradeoff an explicit choice rather than a gut call.

The model in action

In the workbook, each of the 602 songs is ranked from 1 (highest) to 602 on every variable, and the weighted ranks are added together. Because the scores are built from ranks, a lower score means a better fit.

For example, All About That Bass ranked 57th for energy, 107th for BPM, 45th for danceability, and 371st for popularity. Its song score works out as:

That was the best song score in the dataset, even though the song’s popularity rank was only 371st. That’s the model doing its job: fit outweighs fame.

Decision: Treat songs and artists as two separate opportunities. Songs can be sponsored on their own for marketing use, while artists are partnership targets chosen for both fit and reach.

Go-to-market plan

The campaign focuses on reaching listeners in the moments they are working out. That fits the scoring model well, since energy, BPM, and danceability are exactly what people look for in workout music.

Where to spend

  • About 70% of the sponsorship budget goes to the highest-fit songs and artists, and about 30% to one widely recognized artist to drive reach. Because fame and energy pull in opposite directions, this split funds both. (It’s separate from the 70/30 weighting inside the scoring model.)
  • Placements prioritize workout, running, and gym playlists over general pop playlists.
  • Multi-year sponsorship rights: With chart energy down about 17% in a decade, high-energy music is getting scarcer, and longer terms typically come at a discount.

Three launch phases

Phase 1 · No spend

Set up

Finalize the budget split, sign multi-year rights, and sequence the playlist like a workout: warm-up, peak intensity, cool-down.

Phase 2 · Weeks 1–4

Test launch

On a small test budget, launch “Amplify: 45-Minute Session” on Spotify, run audio ads targeted to workout listening using each track’s loudest 15 seconds, and sponsor established workout playlists.

Phase 3 · Months 2–3

Scale

Seed tracks with ten mid-size fitness creators, leading with the most danceable songs, and add a QR code on the can that opens the playlist, linking in-store purchases to the online campaign.

Limitations and next steps

  • The weights are judgment calls. They reflect our reading of the brand, not an estimate from data. A next step would be testing how much the top picks change under different weights, or validating the weights with a survey of the target audience.
  • Ranks hide magnitude. Ranking puts variables on one scale but treats a small gap and a large gap between neighbors the same way.
  • The data ends in 2019. Current listening trends and artist popularity may have shifted.
  • No cost or licensing data. The model ranks fit, not price, so the final selection would need to account for what each sponsorship actually costs.
  • The plan should be tested before scaling. The Phase 2 test budget is the place to compare ad cuts and playlist placements before committing the full spend.
